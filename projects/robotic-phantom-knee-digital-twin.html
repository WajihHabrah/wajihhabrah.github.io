Master’s Thesis: Robotic Phantom Knee Digital Twin

My master’s thesis project focused on developing a one-degree-of-freedom robotic phantom knee test platform, combining physical actuators, embedded sensors, ROS 2 and a Unity-based digital twin for real-time control, visualization and engineering validation.

Project Overview

The Robotic Phantom Knee is a one-degree-of-freedom physical and digital test platform developed as part of my master’s thesis in Biomedical Engineering at Chalmers University of Technology. The platform was designed to support early-stage investigation and evaluation of exoskeleton and rehabilitation-robotics concepts in a controlled bench-top environment.

The system combines antagonistic robotic actuation, an elastic variable-stiffness mechanism, pneumatic soft-tissue simulation, embedded sensing, ROS 2 communication and a Unity-based digital twin. Together, these subsystems enable physical behaviour, actuator control, sensor feedback and the corresponding digital representation to be studied as an integrated system.

A Raspberry Pi 5 running Ubuntu and ROS 2 provides the central communication and control platform, linking the physical knee mechanism with the Unity environment and supporting real-time exchange of commands and system feedback.

Engineering Motivation

Early-stage development of exoskeleton and rehabilitation-robotics concepts requires a test environment in which mechanical behaviour, actuation, sensing and control can be investigated in a controlled and repeatable manner.

The Robotic Phantom Knee was developed to provide such a physical test platform while also incorporating a real-time digital representation. This enables physical-system behaviour and its corresponding digital state to be observed together during development and testing.

The project therefore brings together robotic actuation, mechanical compliance, pneumatic soft-tissue representation, embedded sensing and digital-twin communication within a single experimental platform.

The Robotic Phantom Knee was developed as the experimental platform for my Master’s thesis in Biomedical Engineering at Chalmers University of Technology. The thesis documents the design, implementation and evaluation of the physical and digital system, including its robotic actuation, embedded sensing, communication architecture and digital-twin integration.

System Architecture

The platform uses a bidirectional physical–digital architecture in which a Raspberry Pi 5 running Ubuntu and ROS 2 Jazzy acts as the central integration layer between the Unity-based digital twin and the physical robotic knee.

Dedicated ROS 2 nodes manage communication with the robotic actuators, embedded sensor modules and pneumatic subsystem, while the ROS–Unity interface exchanges commands and system-state data with the digital environment.

This architecture separates hardware-level communication from the interactive digital representation while allowing the physical and virtual systems to remain synchronized during operation and testing.

Antagonistic Actuation & Variable Stiffness

Knee motion is produced using two backdrivable robotic actuators arranged in an antagonistic configuration. Motor 1 acts as the primary actuator, while Motor 2 can track the motion of Motor 1 and provide a controllable opposing interaction around the joint.

The actuators use different transmission ratios: Motor 1 has a 19:1 gear ratio and Motor 2 a 5:1 gear ratio. Their coordinated operation enables the platform to investigate tracking, clamping and compliant interaction rather than operating as a conventional single-motor rigid joint.

An elastic spring-based mechanism is incorporated into the antagonistic arrangement to introduce mechanical compliance. Relative motion within this mechanism allows elastic deformation, providing the basis for investigating variable-stiffness actuation and compliant behaviour around the robotic knee.

Pneumatic Soft-Tissue Representation

Pneumatic chambers are integrated around the robotic knee structure to introduce a deformable outer layer representing the compliant behaviour of soft tissue surrounding the joint.

By controlling inflation, deflation and air release, the pneumatic subsystem changes the deformation of the chambers and allows their mechanical response to be observed during operation of the platform. This adds a compliant physical element that is not present in a conventional rigid robotic test fixture.

Together with the antagonistic actuation and elastic mechanism, the pneumatic subsystem contributes to a more adaptable physical test environment for investigating interaction with early-stage exoskeleton and rehabilitation-robotics concepts.

Embedded Sensing & Data Acquisition

Distributed embedded sensor modules are integrated with the thigh and shin sections of the robotic platform to provide measurements describing the physical state and motion of the system.

Each sensor module is based on a FeatherS3 microcontroller and incorporates an ISM330DHCX inertial measurement unit, an MMC5983MA magnetometer and a TMP117 temperature sensor. The sensors provide motion, orientation-related and temperature data for acquisition by the control system.

Sensor measurements are transmitted to the Raspberry Pi and processed through dedicated ROS 2 nodes, allowing physical-system feedback to be incorporated into the same communication architecture used for actuator control and the Unity-based digital twin.

ROS 2 Control & Communication

A Raspberry Pi 5 running Ubuntu and ROS 2 Jazzy serves as the central computing and communication platform of the robotic phantom knee. ROS 2 provides the middleware used to organize actuator control, embedded sensing and communication with the Unity-based digital environment.

Dedicated ROS 2 nodes separate the main subsystem functions, including communication with the robotic actuators, acquisition of embedded sensor data and control of other physical components. This modular structure allows individual subsystems to be developed and tested independently before being integrated into the complete platform.

During operation, commands and feedback are exchanged through the ROS 2 communication pipeline, enabling actuator states and sensor measurements to be made available to other nodes and to the digital-twin interface in real time.

Unity-Based Digital Twin

A corresponding digital representation of the robotic phantom knee was developed in Unity and connected to the physical platform through ROS 2. The digital environment provides real-time visualization of the knee mechanism while receiving state information from the physical system.

Communication is bidirectional: control commands can be transmitted from the Unity environment to the ROS 2 system, while actuator and sensor feedback are returned to Unity to update the digital representation. This enables the physical and virtual systems to operate as an interconnected physical–digital platform.

The Unity interface also provides an interactive environment for observing system behaviour, issuing control commands and monitoring feedback during development and experimental testing.

Antagonistic Tracking & Clamping

The antagonistic actuator configuration supports a tracking mode in which Motor 2 automatically follows the motion commanded to Motor 1. This allows the secondary actuator to remain coordinated with the primary actuator while preserving the antagonistic mechanical arrangement.

A clamping function was also implemented to constrain the permitted motion of Motor 2 relative to Motor 1. The clamping band can be adjusted from unrestricted tracking toward a narrower operating range, allowing the interaction between the two actuators to be modified during testing.

During the demonstrated tests, the clamping band was adjusted from 0° to approximately ±38°, providing a practical way to evaluate controlled relative motion within the antagonistic system.

Prototype Development & Fabrication

The physical platform was developed through an iterative prototyping process combining custom 3D modelling, additive manufacturing and mechanical assembly. Structural and functional components of the knee mechanism were modelled specifically for the requirements of the robotic phantom platform.

Custom parts were designed in Blender and manufactured using 3D printing, allowing the geometry of the knee structure, actuator interfaces, elastic mechanism and pneumatic components to be adapted throughout development.

The fabricated components were progressively assembled with the robotic actuators, sensing hardware and pneumatic subsystem. This iterative approach allowed mechanical fit, accessibility, subsystem interaction and overall prototype behaviour to be evaluated and refined during development.

Testing & Experimental Evaluation

The platform was evaluated incrementally through subsystem and partial-system testing. This approach allowed individual components and communication paths to be verified before progressing toward more integrated operation.

Testing included robotic actuator operation, antagonistic tracking and clamping behaviour, variable-stiffness mechanism response, pneumatic chamber actuation, embedded sensing, ROS 2 communication and synchronization with the Unity-based digital environment.

The experimental work was used to assess the implemented system functions, identify integration limitations and evaluate the feasibility of the robotic phantom knee as a physical–digital platform for further development and more comprehensive testing.

Applications & Future Development

The Robotic Phantom Knee was developed as a research and engineering test platform rather than as a direct anatomical replacement for the human knee. Its main role is to provide a controllable physical–digital environment in which early-stage exoskeleton, rehabilitation-robotics and human–robot interaction concepts can be explored before progressing to more advanced validation.

The platform can support investigation of compliant actuation, antagonistic control strategies, embedded sensing, digital-twin communication and interaction between wearable robotic concepts and a repeatable bench-top test system.

Future development could include more advanced control strategies, improved biomechanical representation, expanded sensing, enhanced pneumatic control, tighter physical–digital synchronization and more comprehensive system-level validation.
